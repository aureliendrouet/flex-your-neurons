## Part 1 — A "Conditions d'utilisation" / "Terms of use" page

**Why.** The site publishes cognitive tests. Even though it reports no score, a visitor can
plausibly read it as an assessment instrument, and the topic (intelligence, giftedness,
learning difficulties) is one where people take results personally and act on them. The page
must do three jobs: state that this is **not** an assessment or diagnostic instrument, limit
liability, and state the (already true) privacy position. Model:
<https://aureliendrouet.github.io/composer-pour-le-jeu-video/conditions/>.

## Part 2 — Defects

### 2.1 Chronometry: `Date.now()` → `performance.now()`

**Where.** `src/components/Quiz.tsx:111` (`shownAt` init), `:165` (per-item reset), `:193`
(`Date.now() - shownAt.current` passed as `latencyMs`).

**Problem.** `Date.now()` is wall-clock: coarse, and it jumps when the OS adjusts the clock
(NTP, DST on some platforms, a manual change). A jump mid-item writes a nonsense latency — and
possibly a negative one — into permanent history. `performance.now()` is monotonic and
sub-millisecond. This is the standard chronometry rule in browser experiment frameworks
(`docs/LIBRARIES.md` §3, "What jsPsych is right about").

**Fix.**

1. Switch `shownAt` and the elapsed computation to `performance.now()`.
2. Start the clock when the stimulus is actually on screen: inside the existing per-item effect
   (`Quiz.tsx:163`), set it in a `requestAnimationFrame` callback rather than synchronously, so
   layout and paint of the new item are not counted as thinking time. Cancel the pending frame
   in the effect cleanup.
3. Leave `session.startedAt` / `finishedAt` on `Date.now()` — those are timestamps, not
   durations, and the progress page formats them as dates.
4. Clamp defensively where the response is built: `Math.max(0, Math.round(elapsed))`.

**Do not migrate stored history.** Existing `latencyMs` values stay as they are; the unit is the
same millisecond and the change only improves new measurements.

**Test.** Unit-testable only at the seam, so keep it small: extract nothing if it complicates
the component — an `e2e` assertion that a completed drill records `latencyMs > 0` for every
response (readable from the exported JSON on the progress page) is the honest check.

### 2.2 Span latency includes the playback animation

**Where.** `src/components/Quiz.tsx:163-171` starts the clock when the item mounts, but a `span`
item plays its sequence first (`src/components/StimulusView.tsx:600-620`, `setTimeout` chain).
The playback duration — which grows with span length, i.e. with difficulty — is therefore inside
the recorded latency.

**Consequences today.** `medianLatencyMs` for `span` (`src/lib/scoring.ts:134`) is not a
response time, and the overall median (`:166`) mixes it with the other nine formats. It also
means the *harder* the item, the longer the recorded "thinking time", independent of the user.

**Fix — pick one, 1 is preferred:**

1. **Start the clock when the response becomes possible.** The component already tracks this:
   `spanReady` flips true when playback finishes (`Quiz.tsx:108,166`). Reset `shownAt` at that
   transition for span items. This makes span latency mean the same thing as every other
   format's, and is a ~5-line change.
2. Record `presentationMs` separately on the response and subtract it in scoring. More faithful,
   but it changes the persisted `Response` shape (`src/lib/types.ts:230`) and needs a migration
   path for existing records — not worth it for this.

Whichever is chosen, state it in a comment referencing this section.

### 2.3 Documentation claims timing that does not exist

Three stale statements, all cheap to fix:

| Location | Says | Reality |
|----------|------|---------|
| `docs/GENERATABILITY.md` §2, row 11 | "Symbol search **(timed)**" | Nothing in the app enforces a time limit |
| `docs/GENERATABILITY.md` §3, `symbol-search` row | "timed target detection" | Same |
| `src/lib/store.ts:32` | "Suppress the **countdown timer** and any animation" | There is no countdown timer; `reducedMotion` only affects the span animation |

**Decide, then make code and docs agree.** The honest reading: processing speed (Gs) is a
*speeded* construct — the score is how much you get through per unit time. Measuring latency on
an untimed item is a defensible proxy, but it is not the same thing.

- **Minimum:** reword all three to describe what exists ("latency-scored", "reduced motion:
  suppress the span animation").
- **Better (optional, scope it separately):** give `symbol-search` a real per-item deadline —
  a visible countdown, auto-submit as incorrect on expiry, and the deadline recorded on the
  response so history stays interpretable. This is a feature, not a fix; do not fold it into
  this part silently.

### 3.1 "What an IQ test does not measure" (highest value)

Source: `docs/IQ-TESTS.md` §8.1. The site currently explains at length why it reports no score,
but never says what the score would have left out — which is the visitor's implicit next
question, and the thing that makes the "no score" message land rather than read as an excuse.

Four items, as a `limits`-style list: divergent creativity; emotional and social intelligence;
practical/adaptive competence; conation (persistence, curiosity, conscientiousness). Close with
the recursive point: performance on these ten formats is evidence about these ten formats.

Place it **immediately after** `limitsHeading`, or as the first entries of a renamed section —
whichever avoids two lists of near-identical shape sitting back to back.

### 3.2 Ceiling effects and high-range tests

Source: `docs/IQ-TESTS.md` §3.9, `GENERATABILITY.md` §6.2. Short section, three beats:

1. Standard batteries lose resolution above ~145–160; near the ceiling one careless error moves
   the score by several points.
2. A subculture of untimed, open-response "high-range" tests exists to claim resolution up to
   190. Their norms come from self-selected volunteers who spent weeks on the items, and an
   untimed test measures persistence and free time alongside reasoning.
3. Hence difficulty here stops at level 5 and is a *practice* ladder, not a measurement scale.

This also pre-empts the "your hardest items are too easy" complaint, from a position of having
thought about it.

### 3.3 Why the WAIS cannot be put online, and Raven-style items can

Source: `docs/IQ-TESTS.md` §7. One paragraph in the "test families" section: closed-response
tests (Raven's, CFIT, Wonderlic, NNAT, ICAR) are fully machine-scorable; Wechsler-family
verbal subtests are open responses scored against a rubric, and Block Design is observed
physical manipulation. That boundary is exactly the boundary of what this site covers — the
same conclusion the verbal-items section reaches from the generation side, which is worth
saying out loud because it makes the omission look principled instead of convenient.

### 3.4 Difficulty is designed, not calibrated

Source: `docs/IQ-TESTS.md` §7.1, `GENERATABILITY.md` §5. One paragraph appended to
`difficultyHeading`: the bands follow published cognitive operators (a defensible ordering), but
no item carries an IRT difficulty parameter fitted to response data, so the adaptive ladder is a
staircase and not an ability estimate. Also check `dashboard.settingAdaptiveHint`
(`src/lib/i18n/en.ts:147`) still reads as a rule of thumb and not as a measurement claim — it
currently does; do not "improve" it into one.

### 3.5 Trade marks, one sentence

Source: `docs/IQ-TESTS.md` §9. The `limits` entry "Nothing here is copied from a published test"
gains a sentence: test names are used descriptively to situate a format, never as a product
claim, and this site is not affiliated with or endorsed by their publishers. The `seenIn`
strings (`src/lib/i18n/en.ts:179-242`) are already in the correct descriptive register —
**leave them alone**, and do not let a future edit turn one into "the Raven's test".

### 3.6 ICAR, one line in Sources

Source: `docs/IQ-TESTS.md` §3.10. Mentioning the one open, calibrated, properly licensed item
bank — and that it is deliberately not used, because a fixed bank of a few dozen items is
